    Each playbook owns its state machine; no shared lifecycle

    acceptednot accepted

    Every artifact moves only through its own playbook's states machine, written solely by playbook-transition; playbooks hand off at a status, not through a shared lifecycle.

    Context§

    Plans had one lifecycle in core config (plan.statuses, superstates, post hooks) driven by a plan-only transition command, while playbooks drove their own machines through playbook-transition. When groom became a playbook, its machine was designed as its own subset of the plan lifecycle joined to develop's at the edges — "two different subsets of one machine, not one shared monolithic machine". Develop first wrote the shared lifecycle names straight onto status:. A later fix showed the cost of shared names: develop's entry status awaiting-plan-review matched no real plan, because in groom it is a superstate that is never written to a file.

    Decision§

    The shared plan-lifecycle vocabulary is gone. Each playbook declares named machines in its playbook.yaml states: block; those blocks are the authoritative status sets. Playbooks hand off at a status the next one accepts as entry — groom ends at ready-for-dev, develop starts there or at awaiting-approval, where handing the plan to develop is the approval. booping playbook-transition is the only writer of run state and its printed mutation report is authoritative — never re-read to verify; booping playbook-state is the read-only frontier used to resume.

    Per-playbook machines§

    chosencomplexity: mediumreversibility: low

    Each playbook owns and documents its statuses

    Each playbook's states: block is its own machine over its own artifact.

    • Pros: one dispatcher for every machine; a playbook's statuses are read in one file; user playbooks work the same way.
    • Cons: hand-off statuses must agree between two files; the README's Statuses section is hand-maintained narrative over them.
    • Complexity: medium.
    • Reversibility: low.

    One lifecycle in core config§

    rejectedcomplexity: mediumreversibility: medium

    A second dispatcher; plan domain in core

    Plan statuses and hooks stay in src/config.yaml with their own transition command.

    • Pros: one place lists every plan status.
    • Cons: two dispatchers with duplicated idempotency, hooks and reporting; plan hooks unreachable from playbook machines.
    • Complexity: medium.
    • Reversibility: medium.

    One monolithic machine shared by playbooks§

    rejectedcomplexity: highreversibility: medium

    Rejected at the groom port

    Groom and develop drive one machine covering the whole plan lifecycle.

    • Pros: the hand-off is inside one machine.
    • Cons: not named in the source beyond its rejection at the groom port.
    • Complexity: high.
    • Reversibility: medium.

    Consequences§

    • A status a playbook reads from another must be one the other actually writes, not a superstate.
    • Hooks run after status: is written, so a failing hook leaves the artifact advanced; hooks that talk to the outside absorb their failures (adr/0019).
